# Dastgāh & Āvāz

While a single *maqām* establishes one specific **modal ethos** (*eḥsās-e modāl*), extended performance in Persian classical music rarely remains static in one mode. To sustain emotional depth and variety without breaking musical coherence, master musicians organized modulations into multi-modal cycles known as ***dastgāhs*** and their smaller subsidiary counterparts, ***āvāzes***.

---

## 1. *Dastgāh* (دستگاه)

In Persian music, performers can modulate from one *maqām* to another—continuously transforming the modal ethos over the course of a performance. However, changing *maqāms* is a delicate artistic operation: jumping abruptly between unrelated modes produces a jarring shift in modal feeling.

Over generations, master musicians codified the most organic and expressive modulation pathways into structured collections called ***dastgāhs*** (one of the literal meanings of *dastgāh* is "system" or "organized collection").

### Structural Components of a *Dastgāh*
* **Definition:** A *dastgāh* is an ordered collection of pieces called ***gūshehs*** (گوشه), composed in different *maqāms* and sequenced so that transitions between modal feelings unfold smoothly.
* **Mother *Maqām* (*Maqām-e Mādar* — مقام مادر):** Every *dastgāh* is anchored by one primary *maqām* that lends its name to the entire system—for example, *Maqām-e Māhūr* is the mother *maqām* of *Dastgāh-e Māhūr*, and *Maqām-e Shūr* is the mother *maqām* of *Dastgāh-e Shūr*.
* ***Darāmad* (درآمد):** Derived from the verb *dar-āmadan* ("to enter"), the *darāmad* is the opening section of every *dastgāh*. It introduces the listener to the mother *maqām* and establishes the tonal home of the cycle.
* ***Forūd* (فرود):** A characteristic cadential melodic model (*sarmashq*) performed at the conclusion of most *gūshehs* to bring the melody smoothly back to the mother *maqām* (*darāmad*). The *forūd* acts as the structural glue binding diverse *gūshehs* into a unified whole. (In certain cases, a *gūsheh* omits the *forūd* and flows directly into the next *gūsheh*.)

Because it opens in the mother *maqām*, journeys through secondary *maqāms* across progressively higher registers, and repeatedly returns home via *forūds*, a *dastgāh* functions as a **closed modal cycle**.

### Example: *Forūd-e Māhūr* (فرود ماهور)
Each *dastgāh* possesses its own signature *forūd* formulas. In *Dastgāh-e Māhūr*, a classic *forūd* descends stepwise through the lower *dāng*, dips briefly to the leading tone below the finalis, and settles decisively on the mother *maqām*'s resting pitch.

{% include figures/theory/dastgah/forud-mahur.html %}

> **Historical Note:** The *dastgāh* concept did not exist in ancient Persian music. Across the Persian–Arabic–Turkish musical world, only Iranian classical music—and the closely related Azerbaijani tradition—developed and adopted the *dastgāh* system roughly two centuries ago.

## 2. *Āvāz* (آواز)

Literally meaning "sound," "voice," or "singing," the term ***āvāz*** carries two distinct technical meanings in modern Persian music:

1. **Free-Meter Music:** Any non-metric vocal or instrumental performance, contrasted with metered (*żarbī* — ضربی) repertoire.
2. **Subsidiary *Dastgāh*:** A smaller-scale, secondary *dastgāh* derived from a parent *dastgāh*.

In this second (modal-system) sense, an *āvāz* shares the same cyclic principles as a *dastgāh*—it has its own *darāmad*, *gūshehs*, and *forūds*—but on a **more compact scale**:
* Its overall **pitch compass** is narrower.
* It contains **fewer *gūshehs***.
* It frequently **returns to its parent *dastgāh***, reinforcing its familial link to the mother system.

---

## 3. The Canonical 7 *Dastgāhs* and 5 *Āvāzes*

Classical Persian theory organizes the complete modal repertoire into **seven *dastgāhs*** and **five *āvāzes*** (12 systems in total):

* **The 7 *Dastgāhs*:** *Shūr* (شور), *Navā* (نوا), *Segāh* (سه‌گاه), *Chahārgāh* (چهارگاه), *Homāyūn* (همایون), *Māhūr* (ماهور), and *Rāst-Panjgāh* (راست‌پنجگاه).
* **The 5 *Āvāzes*:**
  * Derived from ***Shūr*** (4): *Abū'aṭā* (ابوعطا), *Bayāt-e Tork* (بیات ترک), *Afshārī* (افشاری), and *Dashtī* (دشتی).
  * Derived from ***Homāyūn*** (1): *Bayāt-e Eṣfahān* (بیات اصفهان).
  * The remaining five *dastgāhs* have no canonical derived *āvāzes*.

### Why 7, 5, and 12?
The grouping of the repertoire into **7 *dastgāhs*** and **5 *āvāzes*** (summing to **12**) reflects the deep historical, philosophical, and cosmological prestige of those numbers in Iranian culture:
* ***Bayāt-e Kord* (بیات کرد):** Some theorists include *Bayāt-e Kord* (derived from *Shūr*) as a sixth *āvāz*. However, because it shares close melodic affinities with *Dashtī*, frequently reverts to *Shūr*, and would push the count from 5 to 6 (and 12 to 13), it is often excluded from the canonical list of five *āvāzes*.
* **Other Potential *Āvāzes*:** Several other prominent *gūshehs*—such as *Shūshtarī*, *Mokhālef*, and *Ḥeṣār*—possess enough modal weight and repertoire depth to function as independent *āvāzes*, though tradition keeps them nested within their parent *dastgāhs*.

In the traditional Persian worldview, *maqāms* do not exist in isolation; they acquire their full identity only inside the cyclic architecture of the *dastgāhs* and *āvāzes*. For this reason, the *dastgāh* is the single most important structural concept in Persian classical music.
