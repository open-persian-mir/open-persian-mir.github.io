## Sound Field, Potential Scale, and Actual Scale

### Sound Field (*Bastar-e Ṣowtī* — بستر صوتی)
If all pitches used in a musical tradition (Persian, Indian, European, etc.) are written in order from lowest to highest across its full compass, the result is that tradition's **sound field** (*bastar-e ṣowtī*). In Persian classical music—notated against standard concert tuning and the fretting of the *tār* and *setār*—this field spans from the lowest note of the *oud* to the highest note of the *santūr*. Because this representation is theoretical and rooted in *tār*/*setār* practice, taking the *santūr* or *ney* as reference introduces minor differences in pitch boundaries.

### Potential Scale (*Gām-e Bel-Qovveh* — گام بالقوه)
When all pitches of a tradition are ordered from low to high within the span of a **single octave**, we obtain its **potential scale** (*gām-e bel-qovveh*).

* **Register Independence:** The potential scale abstracts away pitch register (*mantaqeh-ye ṣowtī* — منطقه صوتی), which refers to a specific high or low range on an instrument (e.g., the sixth string of a *tār* occupies a lower register than the first string) or in the human voice. Only pitch-class names matter.
* **Historical Refinement:** Alī-Naqī Vazīrī added two frets to the standard octave layout: $D\flat$ (*re-bemol*) between $C$ and $D$, and $F\sharp$ (*fa-diyez*) between $F$ and $G$.
* **Cultural Selection:** Every musical culture selects a specific subset of pitches within the octave—for instance, European common-practice music uses 12 equidistant semitones, whereas Persian music uses a distinct microtonal collection.

### Actual Scale (*Gām-e Bel-Fe'l* — گام بالفعل)
No Persian piece uses every pitch of the potential scale simultaneously; doing so produces a jarring, incoherent melodic line. The potential scale represents the *total inventory* of possibilities, whereas an **actual scale** (*gām-e bel-fe'l*) is the working subset selected for a specific piece or mode.

* **Heptatonic Framework:** Theoretically, a Persian piece spanning an octave uses **at most seven pitches** per octave, making Persian music **heptatonic** (هفت‌نغمه‌ای). By contrast, many East Asian, African, and Indigenous American traditions use **pentatonic** (پنج‌نغمه‌ای) scales of five pitches without semitones (e.g., $C$–$D$–$E$–$G$–$A$–$C$, comparable to the black keys of a piano).
* **Consonance & Cultural Constraints:** Selecting seven pitches is never arbitrary. A hypothetical set such as $C$, $D${% include accidental.html type="koron" %}, $E${% include accidental.html type="koron" %}, $E\natural$, $F$, $B$ violates Persian melodic consonance rules.
* **Actual Scale and the *Dāng*:** Even a consonant actual scale does not automatically sound Persian. Persian melody predominantly unfolds within a single ***dāng*** (four, or at most five, pitches). Structurally, an octave actual scale is a theoretical composite of **two *dāngs* plus one *ṭanīnī* (whole tone)**, and many pieces operate entirely within one *dāng*.

## 2. Mode (*Mod*) and *Maqām* (مقام)

Choosing an actual scale or *dāng* is only the raw material. A **mode** (*mod*) emerges when three structural factors operate together:

1. **Pitch Collection (Actual Scale / *Dāng*):** One or two *dāngs* extracted from the potential scale (theoretically, a mode can be built from as few as four pitches).
2. **Hierarchy of Scale Degrees (*Selseleh-marāteb-e darajeh-hā* — سلسله‌مراتب درجه‌ها):** Assigning unequal weight and functional roles to the numbered degrees of the scale (e.g., in $C$–$D$–$E$–$F$–$G$–$A$–$B\flat$–$C$, emphasizing degree 5 [$G$] and concluding on degree 2 [$D$]).
3. **Melodic Models (*Sarmashq* / *Olgū-ye laḥnī* — سرمشق یا الگوی لحنی):** Characteristic melodic formulas and motifs that recur across pieces in that mode.

While some world traditions foreground either degree hierarchy or melodic formulas, **all three factors are almost always essential** in Persian music. Modes are never invented arbitrarily; they crystallize over centuries of collective aesthetic practice.

### *Maqām* and Modal Ethos (*Eḥsās-e Modāl* — احساس مدال)
Across the Persian–Arabic–Turkish sphere, a mode formed by these three principles is called a ***maqām*** (e.g., *Rāst*, *Oshshāq*, *Ḥoseynī*; in modern Iranian repertoire, *Māhūr*, *Erāq*, *Mokhālef*, *Zābol*, *Dashtī*). Although the word *maqām* is used less frequently in everyday Iranian terminology today, the underlying modal entities remain active.

Once formed, each *maqām* carries a distinct expressive character and affective impact on the listener known as **modal ethos** (*eḥsās-e modāl*). Every individual piece inherits its core expressive climate from the *maqām* in which it is composed or improvised.

## 3. *Dastgāh* (دستگاه) and *Āvāz* (آواز)

### *Dastgāh*
In Persian performance, musicians can modulate from one *maqām* to another to shift modal ethos continuously. However, unprincipled or abrupt modulation disrupts the modal atmosphere. Over time, master musicians codified smooth, artistically coherent modulation pathways into multi-modal cycles called ***dastgāhs*** (literally "system" or "collection").

* **Definition:** A *dastgāh* is an ordered collection of pieces called ***gūshehs*** (گوشه), composed in different *maqāms* and linked so that transitions between modal feelings unfold smoothly.
* **Mother *Maqām* (*Maqām-e Mādar* — مقام مادر):** Every *dastgāh* has a primary *maqām* that gives the *dastgāh* its name (e.g., *Maqām-e Māhūr* for *Dastgāh-e Māhūr*; *Maqām-e Shūr* for *Dastgāh-e Shūr*).
* ***Darāmad* (درآمد):** From *dar-āmadan* ("to enter"); the opening section of every *dastgāh*, which introduces the mother *maqām*.
* ***Forūd* (فرود):** A cadential melodic model performed at the end of most *gūshehs* to return smoothly to the mother *maqām* (*darāmad*). The *forūd* acts as the unifying glue of the *dastgāh*, turning it into a **cycle** that begins in the mother *maqām*, traverses secondary *maqāms*, and returns home. (Some *gūshehs* omit the *forūd* and lead directly into the next *gūsheh*.)

> **Historical Note:** The *dastgāh* system did not exist in ancient Persian music. Within the Persian–Arabic–Turkish musical world, only Iranian music—and the closely related Azerbaijani tradition—adopted the *dastgāh* framework roughly two centuries ago.

### *Āvāz*
Beyond its literal meaning ("sound," "voice," or "singing"), ***āvāz*** carries two distinct technical meanings in modern Persian music:

1. **Free-Meter Performance:** Non-metric music (whether vocal or instrumental), contrasted with metered (*żarbī* — ضربی) music.
2. **Subsidiary *Dastgāh*:** A smaller-scale *dastgāh* derived from a parent *dastgāh*, featuring a narrower pitch compass, fewer *gūshehs*, and frequent returns to its parent *dastgāh*.

### The Canonical 7 *Dastgāhs* and 5 *Āvāzes*
Classical Persian theory organizes the repertoire into **7 *dastgāhs*** and **5 *āvāzes*** (totaling 12 systems—a grouping likely influenced by the historical and philosophical significance of the numbers 7, 5, and 12). Some theorists add *Bayāt-e Kord* (derived from *Shūr*) as a sixth *āvāz*, though it is often omitted because of its close affinity with *Dashtī* and frequent return to *Shūr*. Other prominent *gūshehs* (*Shūshtarī*, *Mokhālef*, *Ḥeṣār*) also possess the structural depth to function as independent *āvāzes*.

## 4. Key Scale Degrees (*Darajeh-hā-ye Mohem*)

The functional hierarchy of pitches within a Persian *maqām* is defined by five named scale degrees, each carrying a standard Persian shorthand symbol in transcriptions and tables:

| Degree | Persian | Symbol | Functional Role | Modal Importance |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **Shāhed** (Witness) | شاهد | **ش** | Most emphasized focal pitch; acts as the center of melodic gravity. Most *maqāms* have one *shāhed* (e.g., *Darāmad II of Navā*), while some have two (e.g., *Darāmad I of Rāst-Panjgāh*). | **Highest:** Changing the *shāhed* changes the *maqām* itself; it "bears witness" to modal identity. |
| **Khātemeh** (Finalis) | خاتمه | **خ** | Definitive final resting pitch signaling complete closure. True finality occurs upon returning to the mother *maqām* (*darāmad*). Some systems have more than one (e.g., *Afshārī*). | **High:** Anchors structural resolution in the mother *maqām*. |
| **Īst** (Pause) | ایست | **ی** | Temporary resting pitch where the melody pauses while the listener anticipates continuation. A *maqām* may have multiple *īst* degrees. | **Context-dependent:** Shapes phrase articulation and tension. |
| **Moteghayyer** (Variable) | متغیر | **م** | A pitch altered upward or downward during performance due to melodic attraction toward adjacent degrees (e.g., *Darāmad of Dashtī*). | **Context-dependent:** Provides directional inflection and color. |
| **Āghāz** (Initial) | آغاز | **آ** | Starting pitch that sets the opening melodic trajectory (e.g., *Darāmad of Chahārgāh*). A single *gūsheh* or *dastgāh* may use different starting pitches. | **Lowest:** Least determinative of modal identity. |

## 5. Types of *Gūsheh* and Naming Conventions

Every *dastgāh* or *āvāz* contains two functional categories of *gūshehs*:

* **Modally Specific *Gūshehs* (*Gūsheh-hā-ye bā mod-e mo'ayyan* — گوشه‌های با مد معین):** Represent a distinct secondary *maqām* within the *dastgāh* (e.g., *Delkash* and *Erāq* in *Māhūr*; *Zābol* and *Mokhālef* in *Segāh* and *Chahārgāh*).
* **Modally Non-Specific *Gūshehs* (*Gūsheh-hā-ye bā mod-e gheyr-e mo'ayyan* — گوشه‌های با مد غیر معین):** Do not define a unique *maqām* of their own and can be performed across multiple *dastgāhs* and *āvāzes* (e.g., *Kereshmeh*, *Basteh-Negār*, *Majles-Afrūz*).

## 6. The *Radif* (ردیف)

The term ***radif*** ("row" or "order") operates at two levels:
1. The **codified sequence** of *gūshehs* ordered one after another within a single *dastgāh*.
2. The **complete canonical repertoire** encompassing all 7 *dastgāhs* and 5 *āvāzes* arranged in performance order.

Functionally, the *radif* is a **collection of melodic models** (*majmū'eh-ye olgū*) that forms the foundation for both composition and improvisation.

### Instrumental vs. Vocal *Radif*
* **Instrumental *Radif* (*Radif-e Sāzī*):** Broader in overall scope and adapted to instrumental idioms. The most widely taught versions today are those of **Mirzā Abdollāh** and **Āqā Ḥoseynqolī**.
* **Vocal *Radif* (*Radif-e Āvāzī*):** Shares the same modal core, adapted to vocal technique and deeply intertwined with classical Persian poetry (both meaning and poetic meter). The most prominent vocal transmissions are those of **Abdollāh Khān Davāmī** and **Maḥmūd Karīmī**.
* **The Ma'rūfī Compilation (1963 / 1342 SH):** Following an 18-month committee convened by the Fine Arts Administration—bringing together masters Nūr-Alī Borūmand, Alī-Akbar Shahnāzī, Abolḥasan Ṣabā, Aḥmad Ebādī, Roknoddīn Mokhtārī, and Mūsā Ma'rūfī—complete consensus on *gūsheh* names and ordering proved elusive. Consequently, Mūsā Ma'rūfī's encyclopedic compilation (drawing on Āqā Ḥoseynqolī, Mirzā Abdollāh, and Alī-Naqī Vazīrī's writings) was published as a reference edition.
