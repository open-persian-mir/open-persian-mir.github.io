# *Gūshehs* & The *Radif*

Having examined how pitches form scales and *maqāms*, how *dastgāhs* organize modulations, and how key scale degrees govern melodic gravity, we now turn to the building blocks of the repertoire—the ***gūshehs***—and the canonical framework that preserves them: the ***radif***.

---

## 1. Types of *Gūsheh* (انواع گوشه)

The individual pieces that compose a *dastgāh* or *āvāz* are called ***gūshehs*** (literally "corners"). Functionally, every *dastgāh* or *āvāz* contains two distinct categories of *gūsheh*:

### Modally Specific *Gūshehs* (*Gūsheh-hā-ye bā mod-e mo'ayyan* — گوشه‌های با مد معین)
Every *dastgāh* or *āvāz* contains several secondary *maqāms* in addition to its mother *maqām*. Modally specific *gūshehs* explicitly embody and present the unique modal identity of these secondary *maqāms*—each one represents a specific mode with its own *dāng*, *shāhed*, and melodic models.
* **Examples:** *Delkash* (دلکش) and *Erāq* (عراق) in *Dastgāh-e Māhūr*; *Zābol* (زابل) and *Mokhālef* (مخالف) in *Segāh* and *Chahārgāh*.

### Modally Non-Specific *Gūshehs* (*Gūsheh-hā-ye bā mod-e gheyr-e mo'ayyan* — گوشه‌های با مد غیر معین)
The remaining *gūshehs* do not define an independent *maqām* of their own. Instead, they are defined primarily by rhythmic-melodic figures, poetic meters, or ornamental patterns, allowing them to be **shared across multiple *dastgāhs* and *āvāzes*** and adapted to whichever *maqām* is currently active.
* **Examples:** *Kereshmeh* (کرشمه), *Basteh-Negār* (بسته‌نگار), and *Majles-Afrūz* (مجلس‌افروز).

## 3. The *Radif* (ردیف)

The word ***radif*** (literally "row," "line," or "ordered sequence") refers to the sequential arrangement of *gūshehs*. In Persian music theory, the term operates at two complementary levels:

1. **Intra-*Dastgāh* Order:** The codified sequence in which the *gūshehs* of a single *dastgāh* are arranged and performed one after another.
2. **Complete Repertoire:** The entire canonical corpus of all **7 *dastgāhs*** and **5 *āvāzes***, with their *gūshehs* organized in performance order.

Functionally, the *radif* is not a rigid concert score to be played mechanically, but a **collection of melodic models** (*majmū'eh-ye olgū*) that forms the grammar and aesthetic foundation for Persian composition and improvisation.

### Instrumental vs. Vocal *Radif*
Depending on the medium of performance, the *radif* is transmitted in two primary branches:
* **Instrumental *Radif* (*Radif-e Sāzī* — ردیف سازی):** Broader in overall scope and number of *gūshehs*, shaped by instrumental idioms and virtuosic techniques. The most widely studied instrumental *radifs* today are those of **Mirzā Abdollāh** and **Āqā Ḥoseynqolī**.
* **Vocal *Radif* (*Radif-e Āvāzī* — ردیف آوازی):** Shares the same modal core as the instrumental *radif*, differing only where vocal technique (*taḥrīr*) and classical Persian poetry—both semantic meaning and quantitative poetic meter—shape the melodic line. The most prominent vocal transmissions today are those of **Abdollāh Khān Davāmī** and **Maḥmūd Karīmī**.

### Variations Among Masters & The Ma'rūfī Compilation
Past masters each organized and transmitted the *gūshehs* of the *radif* in their own way. These different *radifs* do not contradict one another in their core modal structure; rather, they differ in details of ordering, naming, and ornamentation.

> **Historical Note — The 1963 (1342 SH) Ma'rūfī Edition:** In the mid-twentieth century, the Fine Arts Administration convened a committee of six preeminent masters—**Nūr-Alī Borūmand**, **Alī-Akbar Shahnāzī**, **Abolḥasan Ṣabā**, **Aḥmad Ebādī**, **Roknoddīn Mokhtārī**, and **Mūsā Ma'rūfī**—to review every *dastgāh*, *āvāz*, and *gūsheh* of the *radif*. After eighteen months of deliberation, full consensus on exact names and sequencing proved unattainable. As a result, the encyclopedic compilation prepared by **Mūsā Ma'rūfī** (a *tār* master and leading student of Darvīsh Khān, drawing on the *radifs* of Āqā Ḥoseynqolī and Mirzā Abdollāh alongside the writings of Alī-Naqī Vazīrī) was published in 1342 SH (1963) as a comprehensive reference anthology.

## 4. Synthesis: The Tree of Persian Classical Music

To integrate every concept across these four chapters, Persian classical music can be pictured as a single living tree:
* **Roots:** The ancient historical lineage of Iranian music.
* **Trunk:** The ***Radif*** (the combined body of instrumental and vocal transmissions).
* **7 Main Branches:** The seven ***Dastgāhs*** (*Shūr*, *Navā*, *Segāh*, *Chahārgāh*, *Homāyūn*, *Māhūr*, *Rāst-Panjgāh*).
* **5 Sub-Branches:** The five ***Āvāzes*** (*Abū'aṭā*, *Bayāt-e Tork*, *Afshārī*, and *Dashtī* branching from *Shūr*; *Bayāt-e Eṣfahān* branching from *Homāyūn*).
* **Twigs:** The ***Gūshehs*** (both modally specific and modally non-specific), each drawing its pitches from the *potential scale*, organizing them into one or two *dāngs*, establishing a degree hierarchy (*shāhed*, *khātemeh*, *īst*, *moteghayyer*, *āghāz*) and *sarmashq*, and linking back to the mother *maqām* through the *forūd*.
* **Leaves and Fruit:** The composed pieces and live improvisations created by musicians—all nourished by the modal sap flowing through the trunk and branches of the *radif*.
