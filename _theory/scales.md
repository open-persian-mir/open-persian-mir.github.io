# Sound Field, Scales & Maqām

Before examining the multi-modal architecture of the *dastgāh*, we must distinguish between the raw acoustic inventory of Persian music and the structured modal systems built from it. This chapter traces the progression from the full **sound field** down to a single octave's **potential scale**, the filtered **actual scale**, and finally the expressive **mode (*maqām*)**.

---

## 1. Sound Field (*Bastar-e Ṣowtī* — بستر صوتی)

If every pitch used in a musical tradition—whether Persian, Indian, or European—is arranged in order from lowest to highest across its entire usable compass, the result is that tradition's **sound field** (*bastar-e ṣowtī*).

In Persian classical music, the standard theoretical sound field is notated against concert reference tuning (*diapason*) and anchored to the *radif* and the fretting of the ***tār*** and ***setār***. Its outer boundaries stretch from the **lowest note of the *oud*** up to the **highest note of the *santūr***. Because this representation is primarily theoretical and *tār*/*setār*-centric, using the *santūr* or *ney* as the primary reference introduces slight differences at the registral extremes.

### Pitch Register (*Mantaqeh-ye Ṣowtī* — منطقه صوتی)
A **register** (*mantaqeh-ye ṣowtī*) is a bounded pitch range containing a group of notes. Registers differ from one another in relative lowness (*bam*) and highness (*zīr*):
* **Instrumental Registers:** Every instrument spans multiple registers. On the *tār*, for example, the pitches on the sixth string occupy a lower register than those on the first string.
* **Vocal Registers:** Human voices similarly operate across distinct low, middle, and high registers, which play a major structural role in *āvāz* performance.

## 2. Potential Scale (*Gām-e Bel-Qovveh* — گام بالقوه)

When all pitches used in a musical tradition are ordered from low to high within the span of a **single octave**, we obtain its **potential scale** (*gām-e bel-qovveh*).

* **Abstraction from Register:** In writing the potential scale, we ignore which octave or register a pitch might be played in. For instance, the pitch *Sol* ($G$) can be performed an octave lower or higher in practice; for the potential scale, only the **pitch-class names** matter.
* **Vazīrī's Fretting Additions:** In the modern theoretical layout, **Alī-Naqī Vazīrī (1887–1979 / 1266–1358 SH)** added two pitches to the standard octave: $D\flat$ (*re-bemol*) between $C$ and $D$, and $F\sharp$ (*fa-diyez*) between $F$ and $G$.
* **Cultural Selection of Pitches:** Every musical culture selects a finite inventory of pitches inside the octave according to its historical aesthetic. European common-practice music divides the octave into **12 equidistant semitones**, whereas Persian classical music utilizes a richer microtonal inventory incorporating *koron* ({% include accidental.html type="koron" %}) and *sori* ({% include accidental.html type="sori" %}) degrees.

## 3. Actual Scale (*Gām-e Bel-Fe'l* — گام بالفعل)

No single Persian piece ever uses all the pitches of the potential scale at once—attempting to compose a melody with every available microtone produces an incoherent, grating result. The potential scale represents the *total inventory of possibilities*, whereas an **actual scale** (*gām-e bel-fe'l*) is the specific working subset chosen for a piece or mode.

### Heptatonic vs. Pentatonic Frameworks
* **Heptatonic (*Heptātonīk* / هفت‌نغمه‌ای):** Theoretically, a Persian piece spanning an octave uses **at most seven pitches** per octave (*hepta* = seven, *ton* = tone). Once seven consonant pitches are selected and ordered from low to high (e.g., $C, D, E, F, G, A, B\flat, C$), every pitch in that actual scale can be freely used in composition.
* **Pentatonic (*Pentātonīk* / پنج‌نغمه‌ای):** Many musical traditions in East Asia, Africa, and among Indigenous peoples of the Americas use only **five pitches** per octave. The most common pentatonic scale contains no semitones ($C, D, E, G, A, C$—equivalent to playing exclusively on the black keys of a piano).

### Consonance & Cultural Constraints
Selecting seven pitches from the potential scale is **never arbitrary**:
* **Melodic Consonance:** A hypothetical set such as $C$, $D${% include accidental.html type="koron" %}, $E${% include accidental.html type="koron" %}, $E\natural$, $F$, $B$ violates Persian melodic consonance norms and sounds jarring.
* **The Linguistic Analogy:** Choosing an actual scale closely parallels how human languages select phonemes from the infinite sounds the vocal tract can produce. Arabic lacks the consonants *ch* (چ), *g* (گ), *p* (پ), and *zh* (ژ), while Persian does not use the distinct Arabic pharyngeal pronunciations of *ḍ* (ض), *dh* (ذ), and *ẓ* (ظ). Similarly, each musical culture filters pitch possibilities according to collective aesthetics.

### Actual Scale and the *Dāng*
Even if we extract a completely consonant 7-note actual scale from the Persian potential scale, a melody written in it will **not automatically sound Persian**. One decisive reason is the ***dāng***:
* Persian melody rarely roams freely across an entire octave at once; it predominantly moves within the compass of a single ***dāng*** (four, or at most five, pitches).
* Consequently, the octave "actual scale" is largely a **theoretical composite** made of **two *dāngs* plus one *ṭanīnī* (whole tone)**. Melodies unfold one *dāng* at a time, and many pieces are constructed entirely within a single *dāng*.
* Multiple distinct actual scales can be extracted from the same potential scale, though in a heptatonic tradition each contains at most seven pitch classes.

## 4. Mode (*Mod*) and *Maqām* (مقام)

An actual scale or *dāng* supplies only the raw pitches. To create a **mode** (*mod*), three structural elements must work together:

1. **Pitch Collection (Actual Scale / *Dāng*):** One or two *dāngs* drawn from the potential scale. Theoretically, a Persian mode can be built from as few as **four pitches** (one *dāng*).
2. **Hierarchy of Scale Degrees (*Selseleh-marāteb-e darajeh-hā* — سلسله‌مراتب درجه‌ها):** Numbering the pitches of the scale as degrees (e.g., $C = 1$, $D = 2$, $E = 3$, etc.) and assigning them unequal weight. For example, in $C, D, E, F, G, A, B\flat, C$, dwelling on degree 5 ($G$) and concluding on degree 2 ($D$) gives those two degrees structural prominence over the rest.
3. **Melodic Models (*Sarmashq* / *Olgū-ye laḥnī* — سرمشق یا الگوی لحنی):** Characteristic melodic formulas and turns of phrase that recur across different pieces in the same mode.

While some world traditions emphasize degree hierarchy over melodic models (or vice versa), **all three factors are almost always essential** in Persian music. Modes are never invented by arbitrary choice; they crystallize over centuries through collective aesthetic practice.

### *Maqām* and Modal Ethos (*Eḥsās-e Modāl* — احساس مدال)
* **Terminology:** Across the Persian–Arabic–Turkish musical sphere, a mode formed by these three principles is called a ***maqām*** (e.g., *Rāst*, *Oshshāq*, *Ḥoseynī*; in modern Iranian repertoire, *Māhūr*, *Erāq*, *Mokhālef*, *Zābol*, *Dashtī*). Although the word *maqām* is used less often in everyday Iranian speech today, the modal entities themselves remain the core of the music.
* **Modal Ethos (*Eḥsās-e Modāl*):** Once a *maqām* is established through its *dāng*, degree hierarchy, and melodic models, it acquires a distinctive expressive character and emotional impact on the listener. Every individual piece inherits its fundamental affective climate from the *maqām* in which it is composed or improvised.
